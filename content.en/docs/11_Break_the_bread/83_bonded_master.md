As David fled Jerusalem, six hundred Gittites who had followed him from Gath passed before him. David said to their leader, Ittai, “Why are you coming with us? You are a foreigner, an exile from your own home. You arrived only yesterday. Should I make you wander with us today, when I do not even know where I am going? Go back.” Ittai answered, “As the Lord lives, and as my lord the king lives, wherever my lord the king is, whether in death or in life, there your <b>servant</b> will be also.” [2 Samuel 15:17–22]

Ittai’s reply is an oath, not only “I am with you.” He <b>binds</b> himself to David in life or in death.

(connection paragraph to Exodus, OT <b>What the law actually says</b>) This is the nearest parallel to the servant in Exodus 21:5 who says, “I love my master… I will not go out free.” Ittai was not legally a bondservant, and the text does not say he loved David in those words. But he did choose to stay when he was free to leave, and he called himself David’s <b>servant</b>.

A Hebrew servant served six years and went free in the seventh (Exodus 21:2). If he plainly said, “I love my master, my wife, and my children; I will not go out free,” his ear was pierced at the door, and he served for life (Exodus 21:5–6). Deuteronomy 15:16–17 repeats the same choice: he stays because he loves his master and his household, and because it is well with him.

The decision had to be spoken, public, and uncoerced. It was not the law trapping him. It was <b>love refusing a freedom</b> that would separate him from the household he loved.

(connection paragraph to NT <b>Where the New Testament takes it up</b>) Exodus 21:5 is a law about a <b>Hebrew</b> servant who, when release is due, says he loves his master and will not go out free. The New Testament takes up that pattern of willing service, but only after Christ has first served and purchased His people.  In the law, the master does not serve the servant. At the cross and in the upper room, the Lord does. He takes the form of a servant, washes feet, and gives His life (Philippians 2:7; John 13:1–15; Mark 10:45). 

The Hebrew servant could go free, yet love made him stay. Christ, the true Servant, first loved and served us to the death. Set free by Him, believers gladly remain His bondservants, because the Master is faithful and the household is better than autonomy.

The servant’s words in Exodus 21:5 reverberate through the New Testament: true freedom is found in loving, willing surrender to the Master who first served us.

Because God is faithful and has loved us in Christ, believers gladly call themselves His bondservants.

----
<b>Foreshadowing Christ’s Redemptive Work</b>
• Time-bound servitude pictures the temporary nature of Israel’s bondage to sin and law (Galatians 3:23–25).

• The seventh-year release anticipates the “acceptable year of the Lord” declared by Jesus (Luke 4:18–19).

• Freedom without payment prefigures salvation “without money and without cost” (Isaiah 55:1; Ephesians 2:8–9).

<b>From Bondage to Freedom in the New Covenant</b>
• John 8:36—“So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed.”

• Romans 6:17–18—believers move from slavery to sin to slavery to righteousness.

• 1 Corinthians 7:22—“He who was a slave when called by the Lord is the Lord’s freedman.”

These verses echo Exodus 21:2: Christ ends the term of bondage and sends His people out free.

<b>The New Testament Call to Voluntary Servanthood</b>
• Mark 10:45—“For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve…”

• Philippians 2:5–7—Christ “emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant.”

• Galatians 5:13—“Serve one another in love.”

Once freed, believers willingly become servants of Christ and of one another, mirroring the bond-servant who chose lifelong service out of love (Exodus 21:5–6).

<b>Practical Takeaways</b>
• Value every person: God safeguards dignity in both covenants.

• Rest in Christ’s finished work: freedom is granted, not earned.

• Serve freely: liberation from sin empowers joyful, voluntary service.

• Look ahead: the rhythm of release points to the ultimate Jubilee in Christ’s return (Revelation 21:5).
