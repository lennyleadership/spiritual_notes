Let us consider Genesis 2:15–17, the first recorded instruction God gave to humanity. A common explanation compares God’s command to a parent warning a child not to touch a hot stove, lest the child be burned. In that reading, the emphasis falls primarily on the consequence of disobedience: death.

Today, however, I want to consider these two verses from <b>God’s perspective</b>. Rather than focusing only on what Adam and Eve would lose through disobedience, we will ask what God’s command reveals about His heart, His plan, and His desire for them to live in trusting fellowship with Him.



<b>Observation #1</b>: The phrase "knowledge of good and evil"" is not just a basic recognition of right and wrong. It suggests wisdom and authority to rule and exercise judgement. It is what God did throughout the creation cycle.  He sees what he has made and declares it “good” (e.g., Gen 1:10) or “very good” (Gen 1:31) or “not good” (Gen 2:18). 

<b>Observation #2</b>: Adam already displayed wisdom before the Fall: he named the animals, and he recognized that none of them was a suitable partner for him. He did not choose an animal as his wife, but received Eve as God’s provision.   

Eve also had some moral awareness before eating the fruit. She knew God’s command not to eat from the tree. When she looked at it, she saw that the fruit was “good for food” and “a delight to the eyes” (Gen. 3:6). The issue, therefore, was not that Adam and Eve possessed no wisdom or moral awareness. Rather, they were tempted to take for themselves a full “knowledge of good and evil” independently of God’s command and timing.

The phrase “knowing good and evil” points to wisdom and moral maturity, not just a basic recognition of right and wrong. For example, Solomon asked God for wisdom in 1 Kings 3:5-9.  He says, “I am but a little child,” so “give your servant an understanding heart to judge your people to discern between good and evil.” 

The tree of the knowledge of good and evil represents a pivotal choice.  Proverbs 9:10 says, “The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.” So, there are those who fear God, and there are those who don’t. Those who fear God receive God’s wisdom. Those who don’t fear God become wise in their own eyes.

eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil isn’t about some magical fruit which downloads moral knowledge. It’s so much bigger than that. It’s about whether or not Adam and Eve will trust God to attain wisdom. Will they trust and obey the only wise God, or will they disobey and try to become wise on their own terms?

Those who belong to Christ do not become divine in essence, nor do they become autonomous moral authorities. Instead, they learn to discern good and evil in relationship with Christ and are formed into his likeness.

Jesus fulfills the knowledge of good and evil by being the true human who knows, obeys, and embodies God’s good; by defeating evil through the cross; and by restoring humanity to life with God. He does not fulfill it as autonomous moral knowledge, but as obedient, incarnate wisdom.

God's command not to eat of the Tree of Knowledge was not meant to keep them ignorant forever, but to protect them until they were mature.

Human beings need the life of Christ before they can rightly carry and exercise the knowledge of good and evil. Without Christ’s life, moral knowledge tends toward condemnation, fear, pride, or autonomy; in Christ, it becomes Spirit-formed discernment, love, and obedience.

Those who possess Christ’s life can experience fullness beyond the knowledge of good and evil: not by escaping moral reality, but by entering communion with the God whose life, love, holiness, and wisdom are the source of all true good.

He leads believers into a deeper, redeemed maturity: discerning good and evil through the Spirit, while living from the Tree of Life—Christ himself.

God lovingly allowed Israel’s history under the Law to display humanity’s inability to justify itself. Yet He never offered the Law as an alternative path of salvation apart from Christ. From Abraham onward, God’s people were called to trust His promise; the Law prepared them to recognize their need for the Messiah.

The Edenic “knowledge of good and evil” is not technically either gnōsis or epignōsis, since Genesis was written in Hebrew. Yet, theologically, it is closer to autonomous gnōsis—knowledge seized apart from God—whereas life in Christ brings true epignōsis: an obedient, relational knowledge of God that transforms the person.
