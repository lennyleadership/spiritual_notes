Genesis 2:15–17 records God’s first command to humanity. We often explain this command by comparing it to a parent warning a child not to touch a hot stove. That illustration rightly emphasizes the deadly consequence of disobedience. Yet the passage also invites us to consider God’s heart: what does His command reveal about His purpose for Adam and Eve?

Today, however, I want to consider these two verses from <b>God’s perspective</b>. Rather than focusing only on what Adam and Eve would lose through disobedience, we will ask what God’s command reveals about His heart, His plan, and His desire for them to live in trusting fellowship with Him.



<b>Observation #1</b>: The phrase "knowledge of good and evil"" is not just a basic recognition of right and wrong. It suggests wisdom and authority to rule and exercise judgement. It is what God did throughout the creation cycle.  He sees what he has made and declares it “good” (e.g., Gen 1:10) or “very good” (Gen 1:31) or “not good” (Gen 2:18). 

<b>Observation #2</b>: Adam already displayed genuine wisdom before the Fall: he named the animals and recognized that none of them was a suitable helper for him. He did not choose an animal as his wife rather he received Eve as God’s provision.   

Eve also had some moral awareness before eating the fruit. She knew God’s command not to eat from the tree. When she looked at it, she saw that the fruit was “good for food” and “a delight to the eyes” (Gen. 3:6). The issue, therefore, was not that Adam and Eve possessed no wisdom or moral awareness. Rather, they were tempted to take for themselves a full “knowledge of good and evil” independently of God’s command and timing.

Pilate illustrates a related problem. When Jesus testified to the truth, Pilate asked, “What is truth?” (John 18:37–38). He then declared Jesus innocent, yet handed Him over to be crucified. Pilate recognized an important fact—Jesus had committed no crime—but he did not submit to the One who is the Truth.

That quick departure makes it unlikely that Pilate was sincerely asking Jesus for a philosophical or spiritual explanation. His question may be cynical, weary, dismissive, or politically practical: “Truth? In this kind of trial and political conflict, who can know it?” 

<b>conclusion</b>: Moral knowledge apart from God can become condemnation, fear, pride, self-righteousness, or autonomy. We may recognize standards of good and evil while still refusing the God whose character defines what is good.

God withholds wisdom because He wants people to remain immature. Rather, He gives true wisdom through fellowship with Himself. In Christ, knowledge is no longer autonomous possession; it becomes loving, obedient, Spirit-shaped discernment.

<b>conclusion</b>: The only way to receive and rightly exercise the knowledge of good and evil — and to go beyond mere moral knowledge into the fullness of life - is through union with Jesus Christ.

These verses aligned to the plan God intended to do for Adam and Eve in the garden of Eden.

Pilate responds, Τί ἐστιν ἀλήθεια; (Ti estin alētheia?), “What is truth?” Then, rather than remaining to hear an answer, he immediately goes out and tells the crowd, “I find no guilt in him.”

That quick departure makes it unlikely that Pilate was sincerely asking Jesus for a philosophical or spiritual explanation. His question may be cynical, weary, dismissive, or politically practical: “Truth? In this kind of trial and political conflict, who can know it?” The text does not let us determine his exact tone with certainty.

Did Pilate acknowledge the Truth before him?
Legally, yes; spiritually, no.

Pilate correctly recognized one important truth: Jesus was innocent. Three times in John’s trial narrative, Pilate declares that he finds no basis for a charge against Jesus (John 18:38; 19:4, 6).

But he did not acknowledge the deeper truth of Jesus’ identity—Jesus as the King whose kingdom is not from this world, the revealer of the Father, and the incarnate Truth. He could say, “I find no guilt in him,” but he still yielded Jesus to be crucified. His conduct reveals the conflict between recognizing factual innocence and submitting to truth when it demands courage, justice, and allegiance.

John’s theological irony
John repeatedly presents truth as more than accurate information. Truth is God’s self-revelation in Jesus:

Jesus is “full of grace and truth” (John 1:14).

Grace and truth came through Jesus Christ (John 1:17).

Jesus says, “I am … the truth” (John 14:6).

Jesus comes to bear witness to the truth (John 18:37).

Thus Pilate asks, “What is truth?” while Truth incarnate stands before him. John likely intends readers to see that Pilate’s failure was not merely intellectual; it was a failure to hear Jesus’ voice and act upon what he already knew. One interpretation rightly captures the irony: the judge questions Truth yet does not recognize Him.

A careful conclusion
You could say:

Pilate acknowledged the truth of Jesus’ innocence, but he did not acknowledge Jesus as the Truth. He recognized that Jesus was not guilty, yet he did not yield to the King who bore witness to God’s truth.

That is why Pilate’s question remains searching: a person may ask about truth, recognize parts of it, and yet refuse the Truth when He stands personally before them.

Eve at least has some moral awareness that the tree is good and not bad.

The phrase “knowing good and evil” points to wisdom and moral maturity, not just a basic recognition of right and wrong. For example, Solomon asked God for wisdom in 1 Kings 3:5-9.  He says, “I am but a little child,” so “give your servant an understanding heart to judge your people to discern between good and evil.” 

The tree of the knowledge of good and evil represents a pivotal choice.  Proverbs 9:10 says, “The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.” So, there are those who fear God, and there are those who don’t. Those who fear God receive God’s wisdom. Those who don’t fear God become wise in their own eyes.

eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil isn’t about some magical fruit which downloads moral knowledge. It’s so much bigger than that. It’s about whether or not Adam and Eve will trust God to attain wisdom. Will they trust and obey the only wise God, or will they disobey and try to become wise on their own terms? 

When they ate, they were not simply aware of evil; they experienced evil, to the extent that they became evil—sinners by nature.



Those who belong to Christ do not become divine in essence, nor do they become autonomous moral authorities. Instead, they learn to discern good and evil in relationship with Christ and are formed into his likeness.

Jesus fulfills the knowledge of good and evil by being the true human who knows, obeys, and embodies God’s good; by defeating evil through the cross; and by restoring humanity to life with God. He does not fulfill it as autonomous moral knowledge, but as obedient, incarnate wisdom.

God's command not to eat of the Tree of Knowledge was not meant to keep them ignorant forever, but to protect them until they were mature.

Human beings need the life of Christ before they can rightly carry and exercise the knowledge of good and evil. Without Christ’s life, moral knowledge tends toward condemnation, fear, pride, or autonomy; in Christ, it becomes Spirit-formed discernment, love, and obedience.

Those who possess Christ’s life can experience fullness beyond the knowledge of good and evil: not by escaping moral reality, but by entering communion with the God whose life, love, holiness, and wisdom are the source of all true good.

He leads believers into a deeper, redeemed maturity: discerning good and evil through the Spirit, while living from the Tree of Life—Christ himself.

God lovingly allowed Israel’s history under the Law to display humanity’s inability to justify itself. Yet He never offered the Law as an alternative path of salvation apart from Christ. From Abraham onward, God’s people were called to trust His promise; the Law prepared them to recognize their need for the Messiah.

The Edenic “knowledge of good and evil” is not technically either gnōsis or epignōsis, since Genesis was written in Hebrew. Yet, theologically, it is closer to autonomous gnōsis—knowledge seized apart from God—whereas life in Christ brings true epignōsis: an obedient, relational knowledge of God that transforms the person.
